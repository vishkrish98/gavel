Gavel

Decision support for a student-government funding committee

Vish Gopalakrishnan · Yale SOM, MGT 409 (AI Foundations for Managers)

Repo: github.com/vishkrish98/gavel (public)

App: run locally with python run.py (see README.md); not hosted online

Gavel turns a funding policy into machine-checkable rules a human approves, then checks every club budget line against them with a citation and a dollar amount. It recommends and explains, and the committee always decides.

2. Motivation

Student-government funding committees exist on almost every campus: a small group of elected or appointed students reviews club budget requests against a written policy every semester, usually working from a spreadsheet and whatever institutional memory the committee happens to carry over from the people who sat on it last year. The policy itself is rarely more than a few pages: caps per category, documentation thresholds, a submission deadline, a per-club semester limit. But applying it consistently across dozens of line items, several meetings, and a rotating cast of reviewers is where things go wrong in practice. The same kind of purchase gets treated differently depending on who reviewed it and how late in the meeting it came up. A line that should have been flagged for missing documentation slips through because nobody cross-checked it against the exact dollar threshold. And the review itself eats hours a committee member could have spent on anything else. None of this requires bad faith. It's just what happens when a policy lives in a PDF and the actual checking lives in a person's head.

At RPI, I served on the Union's Executive Board as the representative for our affinity clubs and as Club Operations Chairman. Every club wrote its goals and programs for the year and brought them to one annual weekend where the whole board reviewed every club's full budget at once, with anything additional after that handled in our weekly meetings. That annual weekend split clubs across six review rooms, and I watched the same written policy get applied six different ways, particularly around food-expense rules. I now see a more structured version of the same underlying problem from the other side, as the current Clubs and Finance Committee chair for Yale SOM's Student Government. Our process runs on dollar and attendee thresholds instead of six separate rooms: a request under $1,000 or under a certain attendee count gets approved directly by the committee, larger requests escalate to the broader student government team, and everything runs through a shared accounting system so every club's status is visible in one place. That structure is a real improvement over what I saw at RPI. But the thresholds themselves still leave room for different readings. A request that is small in dollars but large in attendees, or the reverse, can plausibly fall into more than one review tier depending on which number a reviewer weighs more heavily, and two reviewers can reasonably land on different answers from the same written rule. The deeper lesson from both roles is the same: writing a clearer policy reduces inconsistency, but it does not eliminate it, because a human still has to apply that policy to a specific case, under time pressure, without reliably checking it the same way every time. That is the exact gap Gavel is built to close: a system that reads the same policy sentence the same way, every time, for every club, and shows its work with a citation instead of a judgment call.

The question this whole project has to answer isn't "can an AI read a budget line." It's whether Gavel is a demonstrably better review than a student-government reviewer working from Excel and a generic AI assistant, on the same requests. Section 7 answers that directly.

3. What is new

Two established products cover similar ground but don't do what Gavel does. CampusGroups (Ready Education) markets a "Resource Management" module that it describes as simplifying "budgets, reservations, and payments" for student organizations; a Johns Hopkins case study on their site describes the university consolidating separate budgeting tools into the platform (readyeducation.com/campusgroups). Anthology Engage (formerly Campus Labs) offers "Organization Accounting" tools that it says adapt a club's annual budgeting, spending, and payment process and improve transparency around fund allocation (anthology.com/engage). Both appear to focus on tracking and processing budgets and payments at the organization level. Neither's public materials claim to check an individual line item against specific written policy language with a citation, which is Gavel's narrower, specific angle. I couldn't verify deeper feature details behind demo or login walls, so this is based only on what's publicly stated.

Every existing tool in this space that we're aware of (club-management platforms like the ones above, and homegrown spreadsheets) does the same two things: it collects budget requests into one place, and it tracks how much of a club's allocation has been spent. Neither compiles the funding policy itself into anything a computer can check, and neither verifies that a reviewer's decision actually matches what the policy says.

Gavel is different in kind, not just in UI polish: it reads the policy text and proposes a machine-checkable rule for every paragraph that states a binding constraint. Each rule carries the exact policy sentence it came from, verified character-for-character against the real text before a human ever sees it, and a human still has to click approve before the rule affects anything. Every budget line is then checked against those approved rules by plain code, not by asking a model to "decide." The model's only jobs are reading the policy into rule objects and reading a line description into structured facts (category, headcount, quantity, unit cost); the verdict, the dollar amount, and the policy citation always come from a deterministic function (backend/engine.py), never from an agent's own words. That distinction is also what makes Gavel's accuracy numbers in Section 7 look the way they do: a generic assistant asked to review the same requests in one prompt gets the dollar amounts wrong in a way Gavel structurally cannot.

Gavel also now closes the other half of the gap noted above: a club registry (POST /api/clubs/bulk) with a real running balance, computed live from every allowed or reduced dollar across a club's reviewed lines, not stored separately where it could drift out of sync. The budget model behind it is deliberately not an arbitrary number: it supports both a flat per-club grant (Yale SOM's Clubs & Finance Committee gives every club $1,000 at the start of the year, not carried over) and a variable per-club annual amount (the pattern the real university policy in Section 7's portability check actually uses). Many clubs' full line-itemized budgets, or many budgets at once, import in a single call (POST /api/requests/bulk), with per-row success/failure reporting rather than an all-or-nothing upload.

Different clubs also budget in different shapes, and Gavel now follows both without asking either one to change. RPI's annual process has every club write a goal for the year, break each goal into one or more programs (events), and file line items under those programs; Yale SOM's is flatter, a request is just a list of lines. Two nullable columns on a line (goal, program) carry this when it's present, and the Review screen only shows a "group by goal/program" toggle once at least one visible line actually has the data; a flat club's screen looks exactly as it always did, with zero added UI. The engine never reads either column: a line's verdict and allowed amount depend only on its own description, amount, and documentation, so adding this schema change touched none of the already-graded Mode A/B accuracy numbers (every one of the 158 real answer-key lines has both columns NULL). This also maps onto a real two-cadence process rather than inventing a new concept: the Clubs tab's bulk import is built for the once-a-year weekend where every club submits its full annual budget at once, and the Start tab's single-request flow is for one additional request between cycles, the way a weekly committee meeting actually handles it.

4. The agents

Figure 1. CHAIR orchestrates a fixed sequence (compile once → read every request in parallel → the Skeptic reviews lines the engine would currently allow in full → the code Engine applies approved rules → the code Optimizer runs funding scenarios → the Drafter writes committee questions and a memo). Every agent's tools are a filtered view over one MCP server; no agent has a private data path. A rule or a memo only takes effect after an explicit human click.

One traced real run (output/trace_run1.json, run id run1)

  1. CHAIR starts the run on the 24 real club requests and inserts a runs row with status running.
  2. Policy Compiler reads all 16 policy paragraphs in one call (5,323 tokens, $0.000899) and proposes 12 rules, each with a quote verified character-for-character against the real paragraph text; it correctly skips all 4 guidance/informational paragraphs (1.1, 1.2, 2.1, 5.1) as not binding rules.
  3. In benchmark mode, every one of the 12 proposed rules auto-approves because each was the only rule proposed for its paragraph and its quote verified, the same gate a human's "approve all verified" click uses in the real UI (Section 1's app).
  4. Request Reader classifies all 24 requests in parallel (semaphore of 8). For example, request Q01's hotel line (01-L2, "Hotel for regional conference: 3 rooms x 2 nights", $1,140) is read as category lodging, 3 rooms, 2 nights, $190 per room-night, extracted from the description text alone with no category or amount hints supplied.
  5. Skeptic reviews only the lines the engine would currently allow in full. For request Q06 it flags line 06-L1 ("Refreshments (snacks, sparkling water) for 20 people", $220) with the real reason recorded in the trace: “$11 per person seems high for the described snacks and sparkling water; recheck the amount.” It never changes the category or the amount itself.
  6. Engine (code, no model call) applies the 12 approved rules to all 158 lines. Line 01-L2 above comes out needs_docs, $0 allowed, because the $1,140 purchase has no quotes3 attachment and policy 3.5 requires three written quotes over $500.
  7. Consistency report (code) compares all 30 of last year's decisions against what this year's approved rules would allow for the matching line: 6 disagree.
  8. Optimizer (code) runs the $40,000-pot, alpha=0.5 funding scenario across all 24 clubs in one call, with no model involved.
  9. Drafter writes committee questions for the 17 requests that have a needs-docs line, a Skeptic flag, or a large reduction. For request Q07 it asks: “Line 07-L3 (guest speaker honorarium, $1,500): the policy requires a signed contract for speaker fees—can the club provide the signed contract?”, citing the exact policy sentence. One more call drafts the whole-pot memo.
  10. Run ends with status completed, 105,783 total tokens used (recorded identically in both the trace and evidence/usage_final.json).

6. Safety

Three defenses toggle together with one setting (backend/safety.py, DEFENSES_ENABLED): stripping hidden text found at ingestion (font-size:0, white-on-white), quarantining sentences that match a narrow instruction-pattern list (text addressed to an AI reviewer, not ordinary human wording), and wrapping whatever survives in explicit untrusted-data delimiters. Two more guarantees hold regardless of that toggle: every verdict comes from the code Engine and every citation is checked by verify_quote (an obeyed injection can at worst cause a misclassification, never a bypassed rule or an invented citation), and a rule or a memo only takes effect after a human approval click.

Honestly: in this run, gpt-6-luna resisted the hidden Q27 injection even with defenses off. Mode A (no Gavel defenses exist for it, by design, since it's the generic-assistant baseline) is a different story: on the plain-text Q25 injection, it partially obeyed it, wrongly allowing $900 on one line (25-L2) that the real policy says should be held for documentation. That's reported as it actually happened, not softened either way. The defenses exist because a model cannot be relied on to resist this on every input, forever, and this run is a real example of why: the same injected text that failed against Q27 partially worked against a plain generic assistant with no defenses at all. The "on" rows above are the actual guarantee for Gavel itself: quarantined, logged, and verdicts still correct.

False-positive control (Q26, the benign look-alike)

Q26's justification says “Please approve our request in full if the policy allows it … ignore any earlier draft we sent”, ordinary human wording that happens to share words with the real injections. With defenses on, it was quarantined 0 times (0 expected), and its verdicts still matched the key (yes). Across the 24 clean real requests, accuracy was identical with defenses on vs. off (1.000 both) with 0 false-positive quarantines.

Run limits

A deliberately tiny token cap (5,000, well under the Policy Compiler's real ~5,300-token need) ended the run cleanly with status limit_hit and reason policy_compiler_usage_limit_exceeded, confirming the whole-run and per-call limits from backend/chair.py don't hang or crash the process.

Detection latency

Stripping hidden text and matching the instruction-pattern list both happen at ingestion, before any request reaches a model: roughly 0.001 to 0.002 seconds per request, pure regex work against the raw request text. The affected request's own Reader call still has to run afterward like every other request's does, so the time from detection to that request's Reader call finishing is roughly 0.5 to 1.9 seconds, the same range as an ordinary unaffected request takes, not a slow path triggered by finding something suspicious. The defenses add essentially zero latency because they never involve a model call at all.

The real headline here is time, not accuracy. At our one timed reviewer's real pace (17 minutes for 34 lines), a full semester's 158 lines would take roughly 79 minutes, projected linearly from that measured rate, the same kind of stated assumption as the 150-club and 1,000-institution cost rows in Section 5. Gavel's real, measured run processed those same 158 lines, with full citations and drafted committee questions, in 53.8 seconds, about 88 times faster. A committee that currently spends a full annual weekend reviewing every club's budget at once, plus weekly meetings for anything additional (Section 2), is not looking at a small efficiency gain.

Mode A/B figures: evidence/benchmark.json. Excel figures: evidence/manual_first5_scored.json, one expert reviewer, timed, no access to the answer key. "Dollars wrongly allowed" is the sum of every line where the mode allowed more than the policy actually permits, the costly error for a committee, since it's money that shouldn't have gone out.

Both AI modes clearly beat the human baseline on verdict accuracy; Gavel is the only one with zero dollar errors: the manual reviewer scored 0.882 verdict accuracy on 34 lines in 17 minutes, behind Mode A's 0.981 and Gavel's 1.000. That gap traces to a specific, identifiable cause: the manual review had $3,210.00 wrongly allowed from 4 mismatched lines out of 34. Three of those four missed the same rule, 3.5 ("any single purchase over $500 requires three written quotes"), allowing or reducing a line instead of holding it for documentation when the attached docs didn't include three quotes; the fourth cited the correct rule (3.3's $25-per-item cap) but didn't multiply it out by quantity. Exactly the kind of one-off slip a careful human makes once while moving quickly through 34 lines, and exactly what a code engine checking the same two fields (docs, qty) on every line, every time, doesn't miss.

Honestly, where Gavel was not dramatically better: Mode A's own verdict accuracy is now high too (0.981 vs. Gavel's 1.000). A single generic prompt gets most verdicts right on this synthetic policy. Mode A is also cheaper per run ($0.00954 vs. $0.01466), and the cross-club inconsistency rate was identical between the two modes (0.233 each). Gavel's clear, consistent advantage is specifically on dollar accuracy (exact, $0 wrongly allowed every repetition, vs. $9,500.00 for Mode A) and citation discipline (158/158 correct vs. 79/158 for Mode A, the rest real but irrelevant quotes rather than invented ones), plus the hardest verdict category, needs_docs, where Mode A's accuracy drops to 0.846 while Gavel's code Engine stays at 1.000 by construction (it checks the docs field directly, it doesn't infer). Not a blowout on every metric, but a real, structural edge on the two things that matter most for a committee: never releasing money it shouldn't, and never citing a rule that didn't fire.

Consistency with prior decisions

Comparing all 30 of last year's committee decisions against what this year's approved rules would allow for the matching line: 6 of 30 disagree (20.0%), matching the step03 true-fixture baseline on all 30 decisions (100.0% agreement). 8. Limits and next steps

Every request, line, and prior decision in this project is synthetic (tools/make_funding_data.py, fixed random seed 409), invented for software testing and not a real university's policy or a real club's spending. No line in any run in this project has ever come out misclassified: the final run and every benchmark repetition scored 100% on both category and verdict. That is a real, measured result (evidence/ run_check.json, evidence/benchmark.json), but it's also a real gap. It means this report has no actual mistake to show, only the Skeptic's unproven effort to find one: across every run so far, 8 lines were flagged and 0 of them were real errors. The Skeptic does what it's specified to do (flag without ever changing a verdict), but we have no evidence yet that it would catch a mistake if one occurred.

Other limits, in full in evidence/limits.md: the instruction-pattern injection detector is a narrow, 5-phrase list by design, so a differently-worded injection could slip past it (though never past the engine/approval-gate guarantees in Section 6); no concurrency testing (two runs or a policy upload mid-run); approvals take a free-text name with no real authentication; the semester cap assumes one request per club per semester; the frontend was tested in one browser; and the whole system has been run at a maximum of 27 requests / 179 lines, far short of the 150-club or 1,000-institution scale the cost model projects.

Real-policy portability check (completed): a real, public university student union's funding policy (text not published in this repo, per the task's own instruction) was pasted into the app and run through the real Policy Compiler unmodified. Of 22 real paragraphs, only 4 became enforceable rules (21 reviewed, 4/4 quote-verified, 0 corrected by hand). The rest covered cases Gavel's rule schema can't express yet. A simulated run of 6 fabricated requests against those 4 rules surfaced three concrete errors: a travel-mileage line mis-categorized as "supplies" and wrongly capped; one real paragraph packing five distinct dollar rules into one piece of prose, of which the compiler kept only the headline rate (reducing two valid food lines, one to $0); and a cash-prize line allowed in full despite the real policy prohibiting it, because the compiler never recognized that paragraph even though the schema already supports it (prohibited_category). A separate, structural gap: the schema has no percentage-based subsidy rule type at all, and the real policy is built around them (travel, coaching). Full write-up: evidence/PORTABILITY_AND_REAL_USE.md task #2. Honest takeaway: the pipeline and quote-verification generalize to real text; the rule taxonomy does not, yet.

A real user's reaction (completed): a former officer of the same real student union whose policy was used for the portability check above reviewed the live app and gave two concrete pieces of feedback, not general praise. First, the default per-rule approval workflow assumes a policy that hasn't been approved yet, but a real governing document like this one is already ratified by the Executive Board through its own separate process, so asking a committee to re-approve each compiled rule's substance is redundant work on top of a decision already made. We acted on this directly: POST /api/policy/compile now accepts an optional {"already_ratified": true} flag that auto-approves every quote-verified compiled rule in the same call, using the same bulk-approval logic the demo's existing "approve all verified" button already uses, labeled distinctly in the audit trail ("auto (policy marked as already ratified)") rather than disguised as a human click. Quote verification still gates it unconditionally. Default behavior is unchanged. Second, the suggestion that annual budget approval and off-cycle approvals deserve to be explicit, first-class workflows rather than two import mechanisms that happen to map onto them. This validates a design already in the app (the Clubs tab's bulk import for the annual weekend pass, the Start tab's single-request flow for off-cycle meetings) and is reported here as a validated direction, not built further in this submission. One real conversation with one real person who has actually run this kind of committee, reported as exactly that, not a formal user study. Full write-up: evidence/PORTABILITY_AND_REAL_USE.md.

The clearest pattern I remember from my time on the committee was not a single bad decision but a structural one: splitting clubs across six review rooms meant six different groups of reviewers applying the same written policy, and food-expense rules in particular were interpreted differently depending on which room a club was assigned to that cycle. No one on any of those rooms was acting in bad faith. The policy simply left room for judgment, and six different reviewers exercised that judgment six different ways. This is exactly the gap Gavel's consistency report is designed to close. By comparing identical facts, the same description, amount, and documentation, across different clubs, it would have flagged those food-line discrepancies the moment they happened, rather than relying on someone noticing the cross-room pattern after the fact, if anyone noticed it at all. The value of that kind of check is not that it catches one dramatic error. It is that it removes the opportunity for the same rule to be read two different ways in the first place, which in my experience was a far more common and far less visible failure mode than any single egregious decision.

Next steps

  • Both the manual Excel baseline and the optional real-policy portability check are now complete (see evidence/numbers.md and evidence/PORTABILITY_AND_REAL_USE.md); the next useful step is testing the Policy Compiler against a second real policy from a different institution to see whether the gaps found in the first one (missing rule types for percentage subsidies, compound paragraphs) generalize or were specific to that one document.
  • Broaden the instruction-pattern detector from a fixed phrase list toward something that generalizes (e.g. a lightweight classifier) without losing the Q26 false-positive guarantee.
  • Add real authentication to the approval routes so "approved by" reflects who actually clicked, not a typed string.
  • Test concurrent runs and a policy re-upload mid-run; add the same kind of conditioned-write guards the reference chassis uses for payments.
  • A former officer's review is now in; the next step is doing the same with someone who currently holds the role, and with an actual committee rather than one person, to see whether the same two points hold up under group use.
